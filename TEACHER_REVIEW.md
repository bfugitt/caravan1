# Caravan and Sea Routes: review guide for the history teacher

Thank you for looking this over before students use it. I built the game from your textbook's
standards (California HSS Grade 7) and checked key facts against sources, but a history teacher's
review is the real safeguard. Everything below is in `data.js`, so any wording can be changed easily.

## What the game teaches
- **Standards:** 7.2 (Islamic civilizations: trade, the hajj, exchange of learning), 7.3 (China: Tang, Song,
  Mongol, and Ming trade by land and sea; paper, printing, tea, compass), 7.4 (Ghana and Mali: the
  trans-Saharan trade, Timbuktu, Mansa Musa), plus chronological and spatial thinking.
- **Skills:** reading a map, cause and effect (why prices differ), how geography and seasons shape
  trade, supply and demand, and how ideas travel with goods.
- **How it plays:** Students choose a home city, buy goods where they are cheap, and sell where they are wanted.
  On a first visit to each city they read a short card and answer one question. Selling certain goods in certain
  cities unlocks "idea cards." Road and sea events (sandstorms, snow, storms, tolls) involve choices.
  Everything collects in a Merchant's Journal.

## Please check these (highest priority first)
1. **Mansa Musa in Cairo.** The game says a chronicler (al-Umari) wrote that gold's value in Cairo fell after the 1324 visit.
   I verified that al-Umari reports this (a gold coin falling from about 25 to 22 silver dirhams). Historians debate how big and how long the
   effect was, so the game uses a modest drop for about 150 days and says some retellings exaggerate.
2. **Paper.** A paper mill was working in Baghdad about A.D. 794 to 795. The popular story that Chinese prisoners from the Battle of
   Talas (751) taught papermaking to Samarkand is presented as a tradition that historians doubt. Please confirm this fits how your textbook tells it.
3. **Mecca and the hajj.** Written respectfully and simply. Please check the wording, especially for a Catholic school audience.
4. **Era mixing.** Baghdad (700s to 1200s), Mali (1200s to 1300s), the Mongol period, and Zheng He (early 1400s) appear together on one map.
   Each city card gives its era, but students may still blend them. A short class discussion on this would help.
5. **Monsoon timing.** Simplified: ships sailed from Arabia to India with the summer southwest monsoon and returned with the winter northeast monsoon.
   Chinese ships went west in winter and east in summer. The Mediterranean "sailing season" (April to October) is also simplified.

## Facts I checked in sources
- Monsoon wind pattern and Indian Ocean trade (several sources agree).
- Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage, about three months in Cairo, and al-Umari's report on gold.
- Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu built after the pilgrimage.
- Baghdad paper mill (794 to 795), Spain's first recorded paper mill (1056), doubts about the Talas story.
- Zheng He's seven voyages (1405 to 1433) and Calicut as a major hub.

## Facts written from my general knowledge (please confirm)
- Berber and Tuareg guides in the Sahara; caravanserai description; Mongol travel passes.
- Venice's glassmaking and Marco Polo's travels; Quanzhou as a major port and the compass.
- Chang'an as one of the world's largest cities; woodblock printing under the Tang; tea spreading to Central Asia and Tibet.
- Italian cities learning to weave silk; Chinese pottery found near Cairo; paper reaching Timbuktu by caravan.
- Details about Sijilmasa, Samarkand, and Cairo's role as a center of learning.

## Adding more cities
Every city is one block in `data.js` (card text, quiz question, price multipliers, map position) plus one or two routes. The map and price guide update automatically. Each new card needs the same fact-checking as the others.

## What is simplified or left out
- Prices, travel times, and costs are invented to show scarcity, distance, and risk.
- Caravans also carried enslaved people. This is **not** a game mechanic. The last screen says so honestly.
- Wars and conquests (including the Mongol invasions), and the Black Death, are mentioned only briefly.
- Women, farmers, and craftspeople who made the goods are not shown.
